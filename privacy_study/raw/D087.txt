Question 30 (excerpt)

Question 31: How does the guidance address data security?
The guidance addresses data security through sharing information on applicable federal and state
laws focused on data security and student safety as well as the provision of resources germane to
these topics. This information connects district AI practice (e.g., development of AI district policy,
authorized use policies, and informed/responsible use policies) to federal and Illinois privacy and
data-security requirements so readers can understand the legal context alongside the real-time use
in classrooms.

Quick Start FAQ: Prohibited Uses, Procurement, and Embedded AI
Source: page 19

Question 42: How does the guidance identify prohibited uses or do-not-deploy scenarios?
The guidance identifies prohibited uses throughout. In particular, it emphasizes situations in which
AI use should cease immediately, including instances of data breach or compromised student data
or safety, as well as the importance of feedback loops to minimize the likelihood of inappropriate
use of AI.

Question 43: How does the guidance address procurement, vendor review, and vendor use
of district data for artificial intelligence tools?
The guidance treats procurement and vendor review as an evidence-gating process rather than a
simple purchasing decision. It indicates that districts should review contracts, data flows, privacy
protections, retention rules, and vendor practices before approving a tool, including specific
consideration of “who owns what, for how long, and how will data be used” that comport with

district-wide AI policies (e.g., acceptable use policy, informed/responsible use policy, data privacy
policies/agreements, data-sharing policies/agreements, and the like).

Question 44: How does the guidance address governance of AI embedded within existing
platforms, such as student information systems (SIS), learning management systems (LMS),
or assessment systems?
The guidance indicates that extant and embedded AI should be explicitly identified, inventoried,
classified, and governed in ways that align with larger district policies and applicable federal and
state law. This monitoring and oversight should extend to embedded features within existing
platforms as well as stand-alone tools.

Quick Start FAQ: Data Governance, Incident Response, and Security
Controls
Source: pages 20–21

Question 45: How does the guidance address data governance for artificial intelligence
tools, including data entry, retention, deletion, portability, records implications, and student
privacy obligations?
The guidance treats data governance as a central part of artificial intelligence oversight both for
purposes of contracting as well as ensuring the scope of the data governance is shared with
stakeholders. It indicates that districts should reflect the larger suite of district policies and explicitly
define what data may and may not be entered into tools (e.g., what counts as data or records); the
scope of and cadence for collection; data security and retention; and align practice with student
privacy requirements under federal and Illinois law.

Question 46: How does the guidance address incident response for AI-related events, such
as privacy breaches, harmful outputs, bias events, or safety concerns?
The guidance presents incident response as part of readiness for scale and as a playbook need in
contexts such as synthetic-media harms. Districts should align incident response with
implementation readiness and should maintain response playbooks for deepfake incidents,
including documentation, safeguarding, family communication, and supports.

Question 47 (excerpt, page 20)
The guidance suggests that AI used for personnel decisions, governance, privacy review, bias
review, and evaluation should be reviewed for fairness, privacy, and appropriateness rather than
treated as routine automation.

Question 48: What security controls does the guidance highlight, such as district-managed
accounts, logging, access controls, and single sign-on? (page 21)
The guidance highlights security and resilience controls as core governance considerations through
protection against unauthorized access, breaches, misuse, manipulation, prompt-injection attacks,
and data leakage, and through the system durability needed for sustaining operations and
recovering from failures or disruptions. These issues should be described, and their monitoring are
part of the comprehensive suite AI policies a district should develop to ensure data security, student
safety, and transparency with those who are impacted by the use of AI for instructional or
operations.

AI Concepts: Privacy and Data Stewardship
Source: page 55

Explanation
Privacy and data stewardship refer to how districts collect, store, share, and protect student and
staff data. AI tools often require data inputs such as prompts, student work, and usage logs that can
become part of vendor systems. Data stewardship requires districts to minimize collection, ensure
lawful and ethical use, control retention, and ensure transparency with families.

Why this concept is important in light of the guiding tenets
• Teaching and learning are shaped by human relationships and build upon the experiences,
  interests, and assets each child brings into school. Respecting students and families includes

PDF EXCERPT PAGE 7
   respecting their identify as individuals and citizens and not as “data sources” for tool
   development.
• Schools serve multiple purposes—they are places for academic learning and support the
  development of the selfhood and prepare young people for participation in shared civic life.
  Schools model lawful, careful, and ethical treatment of information as part of civic responsibility.
• Artificial intelligence is a means to inform teaching and learning. The fact that a tool can accept
  data does not mean it should receive student data. Educational purpose must govern data entry.
• Informed use of artificial intelligence requires deliberate, context-sensitive, and locally
  determined purpose and use. Districts should define what may be entered, who may enter it,
  what tools are approved, and how privacy risks are monitored.
Examples of uses
• Teachers paste identifiable student writing into a public AI tool for feedback
• AI vendors store prompts and outputs, creating retention and ownership concerns
• Student accounts are created without clear district oversight of data sharing and settings
• Districts adopt DPAs and procurement requirements to ensure privacy safeguards
• Students learn to compose appropriate prompts that are grounded upon ensuring data security.

AI Concepts: Security and Resilience
Source: page 59

Explanation
Security refers to protecting systems and data from unauthorized access, breaches, misuse, or
manipulation. Resilience refers to the ability to sustain operations and recover from failures or
disruptions. AI systems can introduce new risks, including data leakage and dependence on
external vendors for core functions.

Why this concept is important in light of the guiding tenets
• Teaching and learning are shaped by human relationships and build upon the experiences,
  interests, and assets each child brings into school. Protecting systems and information is part of
  protecting the people and relationships entrusted to schools.

Using AI for Instructional Planning: Compliance Note
Source: page 95

Compliance note: A tool is not automatically compliant with FERPA/SOPPA/COPPA/CIPA
because it is “free.” Compliance depends on district approval, executed agreements (e.g., a SOPPA
DPA, where required), vendor terms, and how the tool is configured and used (including what
student data is entered). As with the selection of any AI application within a P-12 public school
district, a district should consult with its legal counsel.

District and Teacher Selection of AI applications (excerpt)
Source: page 139

As districts are asked to innovate using AI in support of teaching and learning there is a need to also
protect students and staff, and sustain public trust through clear, explainable decisions about what
is permitted, under what conditions, and why. Balancing the instructional benefits of AI (or any
educational technology or curricular program) must also include operational concerns including
protecting student privacy—bias, privacy, transparency, and risk assessment and management.
The protecting of student privacy requires a series of district policies, authorized uses policies, and
informed use policies. These policies ensure clarity on expectations on authorized use inclusive of
repeatable routines, defined roles, documented evidence expectations, and, maybe of most
importance, a monitoring cycle that continues after adoption to refine, as needed, those policies
adopted.

Approaching the evaluation of applications for instructional and operational uses is essential
because AI systems can fail in ways that resemble ordinary instructional mistakes (i.e., incorrect
feedback), but they can also fail in ways that create legal, ethical, and equity harms (e.g., privacy
violations, discriminatory impacts, opaque automated decisions, or normalized surveillance).

Three-Step Structure to Ascertain “Goodness of Fit” (excerpt)
Source: page 141

Step 1: Identify tool category and risk level
1. What decisions or outputs does it influence (planning support vs. grading vs. discipline vs.
surveillance)?

2. Whose data does it touch (no student data, aggregated, de-identified, identifiable student data)?

Suggested local risk tiers (raise expectations as tier increases):

• Tier 0: No student data; optional staff productivity
• Tier 1: Instructional support with limited or de-identified data
• Tier 2: Student-facing learning tool using identifiable student data
• Tier 3: High-stakes or safety-sensitive (grading/placement/discipline, student
  monitoring/surveillance, automated risk scoring)
Step 2: Minimum evidence checklist (what must be “true” to approve)
Data privacy and contract readiness: Terms aligned to district expectations; terms of service
(ToS)/ data processing agreement (DPA) review completed; clear data flows and retention/deletion
commitments

Safety, security, and incident readiness: Security posture summary; breach notification
commitments; district incident process defined

Federal and Illinois Privacy, Confidentiality, and Records Laws
Source: pages 168–169

District AI policies and guidance sit on decades of regulation focused upon privacy, confidentiality,
and public records. Most of these laws were written before modern cloud services, social media, or
AI. However, the core ideas—protecting student information, limiting disclosure, maintaining
accurate records, and ensuring transparency—carry over into today’s AI decisions. This section will
share relevant federal and state privacy laws; highlight the differences between a district-wide AI
policy, an Authorized Use Policy, and an Informed use Policy, and why each is necessary; and
provide links to exemplars that can support the development of local polices needed in Illinois
districts.

Within Illinois and beginning in the late 1950s, Illinois’ State Records Act (1957, 5 ILCS 160) and
Local Records Act (1961, 50 ILCS 205) established baseline expectations for how public entities
create, keep, and dispose of records. For school districts, as work moved from paper to email to
cloud platforms—and now to AI-assisted drafting and chat logs—these statutes remain the
backdrop for retention schedules, appropriate disposal, and defensible recordkeeping.

Early federal privacy statutes introduced principles that still guide district practice. The Fair Credit
Reporting Act (FCRA, 1970) emphasized data accuracy and an individual’s right to correct
information used in decisions affecting them. The Privacy Act of 1974 established fair information
practice expectations for government-held data. In education, the Family Educational Rights and
Privacy Act (FERPA, 1974) anchored student education record protections—access, amendment,
and limits on disclosure. These early laws matter for AI as AI systems create new derived
information and can be mistakenly treated as “verdicts” rather than supports for decision-making. So
too, as AI uses the information available for its outputs, accuracy of data is essential to avoid
inaccuracies and hallucinations.

The Illinois School Student Records Act (ISSRA, 1975, 105 ILCS 10) complements FERPA by
defining student records responsibilities in Illinois contexts. Together, FERPA and ISSRA set

PDF EXCERPT PAGE 23
expectations that student records are handled with care, disclosures are limited and purposeful, and
families have rights to access and request corrections. When AI tools touch student
information—directly or indirectly—districts should treat those tools as operating inside this
student-records environment.

Some information categories heightened protections. Illinois’ Mental Health and Developmental
Disabilities Confidentiality Act (MHDDCA, 1979, 740 ILCS 110) underscores strict handling of
mental health information. Health Insurance Portability and Protection Act (HIPAA) privacy and
security rules (1996–2003) shaped modern expectations for protecting health data where
applicable. For districts, these frameworks reinforce a practical rule: avoid using AI tools in ways
that collect, infer, or store sensitive health or mental health information unless the district possesses
clear legal authority, safeguards, and sustained capacity to manage risks.

Illinois’ Freedom of Information Act (FOIA, 1984, 5 ILCS 140) reflects the public’s right to
understand how government entities operate. As districts increasingly use digital systems (and now
AI) to create documents, messages, and decisions, FOIA and records laws raise practical
questions: what is retained, for how long, where it is stored, how quickly it can be retrieved, and
what exemptions may apply. AI use does not remove public accountability, yet it can increase the
volume and complexity of records. That this is the case means that districts must manage its
records with even greater care.

As the internet became part of daily life, laws such as the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act
(COPPA, 1998) and the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA, 1999) elevated expectations for protecting
children’s online information and strengthening security controls in vendor-managed environments.
Illinois’ Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA, 2005-2020, 815 ILCS 530) and Identity
Protection Act (IPA, 2010, 5 ILCS 179) further emphasized safeguarding personal information and
who “owns” this information. This matters for AI because many AI tools are cloud-based, rely on
vendor infrastructure, and can expand the number of systems where personal information is stored
or processed.

Illinois’ Internet Safety Instruction requirement (105 ILCS 5/27-410, formerly 27-13.3) reflects an
evolution from “keep students away from risky tools” to “teach students how to use technology
safely” (See Appendix: Federal and Illinois Privacy and Data Security Laws). In today’s
environment, that instruction increasingly intersects with social media dynamics and AI-enabled
content creation. District guidance can treat AI as part of broader digital citizenship: safety, privacy,
integrity, and responsible participation.

Illinois’ Student Online Personal Protection Act (SOPPA, 105 ILCS 85) responded to widespread
edtech and cloud adoption by setting concrete expectations for vendor relationships and student
data protections. In practical terms, SOPPA reinforces that district should not rely on informal or
“free” tools without clear agreements, safeguards, and district oversight (i.e., who “owns” outputs
produced by AI, and the like). For AI tools, this is often the most immediate day-to-day driver of
approvals, contracts, and required security/privacy terms.

Taken together, these laws form a layered foundation:

• Protect student records and limit disclosure
• Treat sensitive information with heightened confidentiality

• Maintain transparency and proper records management
• Ensure vendor-managed services meet security and privacy expectations

[graphic] Year and Law: 1970 • Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) • Federal law | Scope/Focus:
Regulates consumer reporting agencies’ handling of personal credit information; sets accuracy,
access, and correction rights. | Significance: First major federal statute to constrain private-sector
use of large, centralized personal data files and grant individuals’ rights over that data. | Context:
Pre-internet era; focused on paper and early computerized credit files. Later applied to online credit
reporting and scoring systems. | Context with Internet/Social/AI Threading: Originally aimed at
paper and early computerized credit files. Today it applies to online credit reporting, scoring
algorithms, and tools that may incorporate education and employment histories—raising questions
about how AI-DRIVEN decision systems intersect with long-standing FCRA rights.

[graphic] Year and Law: 1974 • Privacy Act of 1974 • Federal law | Scope/Focus: Governs how U.S.
federal agencies collect, maintain, use, and disclose personal information in systems of records. |
Significance: First comprehensive federal statute to impose fair information practice principles on
government-held personal data. | Context: Adopted in an era of mainframes and early databases,
well before internet-era web portals and online services. | Context with Internet/Social/AI Threading:
Does not mention the internet, social media, or AI, but its fair-information principles now apply to
federal WEBSITES, online benefit portals, and internal ANALYTICS and AI deployments that rely on
federal systems of records about individuals.

[graphic] Year and Law: 1974 • Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) • Federal law |
Scope/Focus: Protects the privacy of student education records; limits disclosure without consent;
grants access and amendment rights. | Significance: First federal law specifically devoted to privacy
and access rights for student education records. | Context: Enacted long before widespread school
networking or internet access; later applied to digital student information systems and learning
platforms. | Context with Internet/Social/AI Threading: Written without explicit internet or AI
language but now governs access and disclosure of records in online student information systems,
learning platforms, and, increasingly, AI-enabled tools that use education records.

Appendix: Student Selfhood, Engagement, and Agency in
AI-Mediated Schoolwork — Surveillance and Data (excerpt)
Source: pages 329–330

• Algorithmic surveillance reduces perceived autonomy and can impair performance: Participants
  under algorithmic (vs. human) surveillance reported less autonomy, performed worse in some
  tasks, and reported higher resistance intentions; framing surveillance as developmental
  (informational) mitigated negative effects. (Schlund et al. 2024)
• Online proctoring can feel invasive and coercive: Students report concerns about the amount
  and personal nature of information shared with proctoring companies; institutional power
  dynamics and trust in the institution may suppress open opposition, which is an agency red flag.
  (Balash et al. 2021)
• Learning analytics can create “datafied” student identities: Analytics often requires linking data to
  individuals and can expand to broad data scope; without safeguards, students can be treated as
  objects of prediction rather than participants with voice and choice. (Rubel & Jones 2016);
  (Prinsloo & Slade 2015)
• Minimize surveillance and provide opt-outs or alternatives where feasible: Learning analytics
  ethics emphasizes autonomy interests; opt-out structures and meaningful choice reduce student
  vulnerability and protect agency. (Rubel & Jones 2016); (Prinsloo & Slade 2015)
