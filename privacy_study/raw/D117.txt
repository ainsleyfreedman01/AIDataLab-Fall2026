Benefits and Risks of AI in Education (excerpt)
Source: page 4

• Data collection and monitoring: Tools that collect extensive student information, including
  some assessment and monitoring systems, may create privacy and security risks and may
  generate detailed records of student activity beyond what the original purpose requires.
• Developmental and ethical concerns: Student age, maturity, learning goals, and the sensitivity
  of the activity should inform whether and how an AI tool is used.

Core Principles for AI Integration: Protect Student Data
Source: page 5

4. Protect Student Data: Districts should establish clear processes for reviewing and approving AI
tools, minimizing the collection and sharing of student information, and communicating permitted
uses. Educators should use locally approved tools and avoid entering protected or sensitive
information unless specifically authorized. Local review processes should account for applicable
federal and Montana privacy requirements, vendor data practices, security, and potential bias.

Guidance for Educator Use (excerpt)
Source: page 5

Districts should evaluate whether a tool produces reliable results, protects sensitive information, and
creates meaningful time savings in local practice.

• Routine drafting and formatting: Preparing draft newsletters, general communications, forms,
  and other materials without entering protected information into an unapproved tool.

Getting Started: Three Immediate Actions (excerpts)
Source: pages 9–10

Moving from principles to daily practice requires local processes that support educators, protect
students, and honor Montana's tradition of local control.

AI decisions should not fall solely to an IT department or a single administrator. Because AI affects
instruction, data privacy, procurement, accessibility, student conduct, and community expectations,
districts should establish a cross-functional team or comparable local process to guide
implementation.

• Technology staff: Review vendor data practices, security, system compatibility, and technical
  controls.

The Team's Work: Depending on local governance and assigned authority, the team may review
state guidance, recommend local policy, identify AI features already present in district software,
support tool review, and help maintain an approved-tool list.

Professional learning should extend beyond tool demonstrations to address AI literacy, instructional
purpose, data privacy, accessibility, verification, and appropriate uses for routine work.

• Communicate approved uses: Tell students, families, and employees which tools and uses are
  locally approved and where they can ask questions.
• Create opportunities for discussion: Explain why the district is considering AI, what
  safeguards apply, how student information is protected, and how the district will respond to
  misuse, including malicious synthetic media and violations of academic integrity expectations.

Policy and Governance (excerpts)
Source: pages 11–12

In the context of AI, tribes maintain jurisdiction over their data and digital materials, possess
authority to regulate technology that affects their communities, and determine whether and how AI
systems interact with their cultural knowledge. Those developing AI policy must recognize tribal
authority and engage in meaningful consultation when state initiatives could affect tribal
communities, cultural materials, or data-sovereignty rights.

Legal Considerations and Liability
AI use can create legal and operational responsibilities involving privacy, accessibility, procurement,
student conduct, intellectual property, records, and other areas. District leaders should use
established review processes and seek qualified advice when a proposed use presents legal or
liability questions.

Safety, Privacy, and Responsible Procurement
Source: pages 13–14

Districts need a consistent review process to protect students, meet applicable requirements, and
manage local risk. The following guidance applies to every AI tool under consideration, including
pilots and free versions.

Part A: Vendor Vetting Checklist
Districts should not deploy an AI tool unless the local review process verifies each of the following
criteria:

• Privacy and data protection: The tool and its proposed use must comply with the Family
  Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) and other applicable federal and Montana
  student-privacy requirements. Review what information the tool collects, how it is used, whether
  it is shared, how long it is retained, and how it can be deleted.
• Security: The vendor should provide evidence of appropriate security practices,
  incident-response procedures, and notification terms. Districts should understand which
  responsibilities belong to the vendor and which remain local.

• Algorithmic bias: Districts should require vendors to provide available documentation about
  how the system has been evaluated for bias.
• Age, notice, and consent: The proposed use must comply with the Children's Online Privacy
  Protection Act (COPPA), vendor age restrictions, and applicable federal, Montana, and local
  requirements for family notice, consent, and school authorization. Districts should communicate
  approved tools and their data practices to families, consistent with Getting Started: Three
  Immediate Actions.
• Contract and data-privacy terms: The vendor must sign the Montana Student Data Privacy
  Agreement or an equivalent agreement approved through the district's established review
  process.
The Frontier Learning Lab periodically reviews tools for use in Montana schools. Tools that
complete this process and meet MTDA and University of Montana procurement standards are
available through the MTDA Catalog.

Part B: Deepfake and Synthetic Media Protocol (excerpt)
• Support for the targeted person: The district's first response must center the safety, privacy,
  and support needs of the targeted student or employee. Districts must provide clear reporting
  pathways designed to reduce retaliation, unnecessary redistribution, and further public exposure.
Part C: Companion and Conversational AI (excerpt)
• Understand the distinct risks: Conversational systems may provide inaccurate or harmful
  guidance, respond inappropriately to disclosures about self-harm or abuse, encourage prolonged
  emotional reliance, or collect sensitive personal information a student would not otherwise share.
• Communicate with families: Districts should include conversational AI in family communication
  about approved tools, local expectations, and how to raise a concern.

Implementation Roadmap (excerpts)
Source: pages 15–17

• Review existing policies: Determine whether academic-integrity, acceptable-use, privacy,
  grading, records, accessibility, employee-use, and digital-citizenship policies adequately address
  AI.
• Use consistent principles: Connect local policy to privacy, transparency, accountability,
  universal access, the human in the loop, and academic integrity.
• Apply the vendor-vetting checklist: Use the review process in Safety, Privacy, and
  Responsible Procurement for every tool under consideration. Do not enter personally identifiable
  information into an unapproved tool.
• Provide role-specific learning: Address AI literacy, instructional purpose, privacy, accessibility,
  verification, and practical strategies relevant to each role.
• Reflect on Local Experience: Periodically review educator and student experience, observed
  instructional value, implementation problems, privacy or safety incidents, and whether continued
  use remains worthwhile. Districts should avoid collecting new student-level data solely to monitor
  AI implementation unless a clear local need and appropriate safeguards justify it.
