Principle #1: Opportunities With Student Data
Source: page 11

Caution: Never put student data into an AI system unless the district’s IT department has vetted that
system and has authorized its use in this way.

While most systems today don’t interact directly with district data systems, this will change in the
next few years, with SIS (Student Information System), LMS (Learning Management System) and
MTSS (Multi-Tiered System of Supports) platforms building AI integrations directly into their
systems enabling new tools for teachers and administrators that leverage rich and up to date
information about students.

When to Avoid Using AI (excerpt)
Source: page 13

• Legal or compliance documentation – Districts should seek legal advice before approving AI
  usage for IEP goals, 504 plans, or mandated reporting situations.
• Medical-related information – Unless the IT Department has specifically vetted an AI solution for
  HIPAA compliance, staff should avoid entering any medical-related information into an AI
  system.

Principle #2: Personalized Learning, Accessibility and Special
Education Documentation (excerpts)
Source: pages 15–16

• Note-taking and live captioning: AI can be utilized for live captioning and for notetaking
  purposes. When utilizing these technologies, work with district technology staff to identify
  solutions that meet the privacy needs of schools, as many of these solutions are not built
  specifically for education.
In addition to these uses, AI can be used to help with special education documentation (e.g. IEPs,
504 plans); however, using AI in this way presents significant concerns that should be addressed
before implementation. IEPs and 504 plans are the foundations on which special education services
stem from, and using AI poses a danger of losing essential professional assessment and breaching
of personally identifiable information (PII) protection.

The most important step in using AI tools in this context is to have human oversight every step of
the way. For example, IEP goal generation can be done using AI, but the prompt and content to
generate the goal requires a secure FERPA compliant system and should be reviewed by the IEP
team for relevance to the student’s capabilities and needs.

Risk Mitigation Strategies: Risk of Doing Nothing (excerpt)
Source: page 22

Staff may utilize public AI systems in ways that risk data privacy.

Data Privacy
Source: pages 25–26

Schools need to protect student data, conforming to both FERPA and COPPA. In addition,
generative AI uses data to train AI systems, and schools should be concerned about the intellectual
property of students being utilized to train these systems.

Providing teachers and students with approved, secure AI systems is one of the best ways to
protect student data. A separate section of this guide covers tool vetting in depth. When schools do
not provide official tools and clear instructions for these tools, teachers and students will be tempted
to utilize public AI systems instead.

It is not enough to remove student-identifiable information before submitting student work to an AI
system. This still exposes the student’s intellectual property to the system. SU/SDs should have a
privacy agreement with vendors that include promises not to train AI models based on chat
conversations. This is the case for education-specific AI systems as well as generic systems with AI
versions (Gemini for Education).

Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) data (medical data) is a particular
concern, as it has requirements that are not a part of FERPA or COPPA. Most educational AI
systems have not been designed to comply with HIPAA. In many cases, it is simpler for schools to
avoid the use of AI for HIPAA protected data. In the future, it is likely that there will be use cases
that involve the use of AI, but initially, it may be simpler for schools to avoid the use of AI with
HIPAA protected data.

Tool Vetting Framework (excerpts)
Source: pages 34–36

In general, approving an AI tool for use in a school district is very similar to any other educational
technology tool that can store FERPA protected data. These systems do introduce some unique
challenges, however. The Future of Privacy Forum released a guide that goes into some of the
unique challenges of these tools: Vetting Generative AI Tools for Use in Schools. (Sallay, 2024)

Generic Chatbots
Google trained an LLM AI specifically for education, LearnLM, and their AI is covered by their
educational privacy policy (when using school accounts only) which gives it an advantage over other
generic chatbots. …

Google is providing some auditing features with Gemini. Unfortunately, as of this document’s
release, they are quite limited and require a Google Administrator. This means that teachers are
unable to have full access to all of the conversations that their students have with Gemini. Even for
a Google Admin, most of the access is through Google Vault and only applies to the standalone
Gemini app and not where the app is embedded into other Google tools. IT departments need to
actively set the conversation retention settings and teachers need to clearly understand which
conversation should not occur in Gemini in apps because of the lack of retention/logging.

Generic Chatbots for Teachers
Providing teachers with access to an official AI tool can mitigate the risks of teachers using public
chatbots with student protected data.

• Privacy being violated through the use of insecure systems or oversharing of generated notes.

Frequently Asked Questions: Which AI tools can I use in my
classroom? (excerpt)
Source: page 38

Most supervisory unions and districts maintain an approved list of software that can be used in the
classroom and that store student data. FERPA, COPPA and other applicable laws govern how
student data must be handled. Most districts have signed data privacy agreements with specific
vendors and require that teachers utilize these vendors platforms. You should reach out to your
district technology team to determine which AI tools you may use. This guide includes information
on how to properly vet and approve AI applications for use in districts.
